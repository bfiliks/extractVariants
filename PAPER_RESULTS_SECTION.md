## Results

The ExtractV pipeline described above was designed in 2022, before the team had the computational skillset to build it as specified. What follows is what happened when the underlying question — can Dickinson's variant taxonomy actually be applied computationally to real manuscript data — was finally tested, three years later, against the complete critical apparatus for Fascicle 34.

The method departed from the originally-envisioned pipeline in one respect worth stating plainly: rather than a custom Gothenburg-model collation system with rule-based part-of-speech tagging, classification was performed by a large language model, given only the taxonomy's own prose definitions and each base-text/variant pair, with no access to any answer key. This is a different computational method than the one specified in 2022, not the one built — a methods choice made possible by tools that did not exist in comparable form when ExtractV was first designed, and one future revisions of this paper should characterize accordingly.

**All 84 documented variant readings across Fascicle 34's seventeen poems were classified against our own two-level taxonomy.** Three poems (F763, F765, and F770) were confirmed, directly from the Emily Dickinson Archive's own manuscript apparatus, to carry no variants at all — consistent with our own 2022 field notes. Of the 84 readings that do exist, the classifier's Level 1 distribution was: repetitives 30%, amplifiers 25%, antithesis 21%, uncategorized 7%, additions 2%, and — notably — 14% fell to an undefined sixth category, described by the classifier itself as plain substitution not clearly fitting any of our five defined types.

**That sixth category is the paper's most important negative result.** Plain, roughly equal-status word-for-word substitution — Dickinson offering "Would" beside "Could," "Root" beside "Core" — is not one of the five categories (repetitives, amplifiers, antithesis, additions, uncategorized) we defined in this paper. It appeared in every one of four independent classification passes run against different subsets of the data, at rates ranging from 14% to 56% depending on how the classification task itself was specified. We report that range rather than a single number deliberately: the instability itself is evidence that our taxonomy, however useful for describing *some* of what Dickinson's variants do, was never built to name the single largest thing they do most often, and no single test run should be mistaken for having pinned down exactly how large that gap is.

**A second result speaks more directly to the paper's original question — do we choose one variant over the other, or hold both open.** Splitting all 84 readings along the taxonomy's own Level 2 axis (does the poem give enough information to choose definitively between base text and variant, or not), 78 readings (93%) gave the classifier no textual basis to prefer one wording over the other. Only 4 readings (5%) were resolved by surrounding context, and 2 (2%) were additions rather than true alternatives. This is close to an exact computational confirmation of Cameron's own claim that Dickinson's variants "extend the text's identity in ways that make it seem potentially limitless" (Cameron 6) — tested here not as a qualitative reading of a handful of examples, but as a measurable property of the complete apparatus for one fascicle.

**A third, smaller result concerns the archive itself rather than Dickinson's manuscripts.** Cross-referencing our own 2022 hand documentation of Fascicle 34 against a full-corpus extraction performed the same year found approximately 87% recall — most of the missing 13% attributable to variant types (phrase-length alternates spanning two lines, base-less additions, alternate manuscript witnesses) that a word-level extraction was never built to catch, rather than errors in the underlying transcription. One clear content error was found and is worth naming for anyone reusing this data: a reading recorded as "Sun→sustain" should read "Sun→Noon."

These results answer the question this project asked in 2022 more completely than the project itself expected to when it began: yes, the taxonomy can be applied computationally to real, complete manuscript data — and applying it changes the taxonomy at least as much as it classifies the poems.
